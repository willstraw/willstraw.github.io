Pacejka Magic Formula 5.2 Tyre Model

During the 2026 design cycle I built a tyre model for my university’s FSAE team. The model is written in MATLAB and uses the Pacejka Magic Formula, specifically the PAC2002 version, or MF 5.2, and is fitted to test data from the FSAE Tire Test Consortium at Calspan. An older model of ours was very limited: it only covered cornering force, didn’t include camber and provided very few outputs. My rebuilt model covers lateral force, longitudinal force and combined slip, all with camber, across a variety of pressures.

The hardest part was combined slip analysis, which looks at how much grip the tyre loses when it’s braking or accelerating while it’s also cornering, or vice versa. The drive and brake tests only used positive slip angles of 0, 3 and 6 degrees, which isn’t enough to model a tyre accurately. My first model matched the data almost perfectly, but a second look at the test points showed clear issues. Longitudinal force was three times stronger in one direction of cornering than the other, and past about 14 degrees of slip angle, braking actually pushed the tyre forwards (I’m still not sure how I managed that). This was fixed by holding the terms the data couldn’t support at zero and adding limits that keep the curves physically capped, at almost no cost in accuracy.

The biggest thing I learned is that a good R² doesn’t immediately mean a good model. My fits were all between about 0.97 and 0.999, but that only tells you the model matches the data where there is data to match. A lap sim, for example, uses the model everywhere. Taking the testing conditions into account was just as important. The test belt is like sandpaper, gripping more than any real track would. The next step is to correlate the model against our own track data and work out how much to scale the grip down.
